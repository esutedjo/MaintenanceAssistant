# Proving Every Number Sets Cleris Apart

> Research report, 26 Oct 2026, compiled for the hackathon from public sources (links inline) and this repository's evaluation. Cleris figures are measured on NASA C-MAPSS simulated engines with synthetic maintenance data; vendor capabilities are as published, not independently tested.

This research found no product that publicly does what Cleris does as a package. Cleris produces one engine stay-or-remove brief (action, deadline, two remaining-life estimates with a calibrated range, task, parts and hangar slot), ties every claim to an evidence ID, and lets deterministic code, not the language model, decide whether the brief passes before an engineer signs. The similar solutions fall into five groups. Engine-OEM health monitoring (GE, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney) sends alerts. Airframer and data platforms (Airbus Skywise on Palantir, Boeing Dataliner, Palantir AIP at FTAI) provide fleet-wide data and AI. M&E suites (IFS, Ramco, TRAX, Veryon, AVIATAR) now carry built-in AI. Cross-industry copilots (IBM Maximo Assistant, Siemens Senseye, Cognite Atlas AI, C3 AI) and startups (Fleetcraft, Zymbly, ePlane AI, Aerogility) make up the rest. Each covers one slice of the job: alerts, document Q&A, AD/SB processing, procurement or shop-visit planning. Two products overlap most and limit what Cleris can claim. Rolls-Royce's Optimiser is an internal multi-agent shop-visit planner with engine, workscope, facility and supplier agents. ePlane AI's AeroGenie writes visible SQL over existing ERPs without moving data. Because of them, Cleris cannot claim to be the first multi-agent engine planner or the only layer that sits on existing systems. What Cleris can defend is narrower and stronger: it measures how well its checks work. Its code caught **311 of 311 seeded faulty briefs** with zero false alarms, **100% of 2,907 claims** link to evidence that resolves, and **all 45 engines that fail within 15 days** were sent for removal, swap or repair. The team's current competitor line points the right way but can be rebutted, because Senseye and Cognite both connect to existing data, and for a shop that runs Maximo, Maximo is the existing system. Every result comes from a six-day hackathon prototype with no certification, running on NASA's simulated C-MAPSS engines and synthetic maintenance data. The pitch should therefore sell a method whose checks can be verified, not proven value in the field.

## Five incumbent groups each own one slice of the engine decision

**Engine OEMs** hold the deepest data on the engines Cleris reasons about, and they deliver it as monitoring plus human judgement. GE says it continuously monitors "44K+" commercial engines (the same page also says "30K ESNs Monitored") and issues 18K+ Customer Notification Reports a year. Standard diagnostics are free under a General Terms Agreement, and "Collaborative Insight" is a paid tier on top ([GE Aerospace](https://www.geaerospace.com/collaborative-insight)). GE's one removal-timing product, ABM.ai, combines an analytics model with on-wing borescope images. GE describes it as "~80% accuracy ... decision always vetted by the human" ([GE 2025 Product & Technology Update](https://www.geaerospace.com/sites/default/files/2025-ge-aerospace-product-and-technology-update.pdf)). Rolls-Royce EHM covers all RB211 and Trent engines and recommends specific Fault Isolation Manual or Maintenance Manual tasks ([Rolls-Royce](https://www.rolls-royce.com/products-and-services/civil-aerospace/services/ehm.aspx)). Pratt & Whitney's ADEM analyses more than 10,000 engines for 140 customers, covering only the GTF and V2500 ([Pratt & Whitney](https://www.rtx.com/en/prattwhitney/services/enginewise/intelligence)). The pattern holds across all three: **each OEM advises only on its own engines, the explanation comes from a human diagnostic engineer or a link to a manual task, and none publishes a remaining-life interval**. Most of GE's newer AI points inward at GE's own shops: a workscope-forecasting "AI-Material Assistant", a certification-document agent, and a records tool at its Celma site ([GE deck](https://www.geaerospace.com/sites/default/files/2025-ge-aerospace-product-and-technology-update.pdf); [Q3 2026 call summary](https://www.tradingkey.com/news/transcripts/262226613-tradingkey)).

**Airframers and data platforms** aim at fleet-wide data layers. In April 2026 Airbus made Skywise a separate subsidiary of about 750 staff, with more than 12,000 connected aircraft and an explicit aim to serve non-Airbus fleets ([Skywise](https://www.skywise.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-airbus-unveils-skywise-subsidiary-integrating-navblue-and-skywise-digital-solutions)). Its Fleet Performance+ product uses NLP to estimate a "most probable cause" for repeat faults ([Airbus](https://aircraft.airbus.com/en/services/enhance/skywise-digital-solutions/skywise-fleet-performance)). Boeing launched Dataliner with Microsoft on 20 Oct 2026. Under Dataliner, airline data "stays in each airline's own environment", and it underpins a new "TechOps AI Agent" whose scope, evidence model and oversight Boeing has not disclosed ([AviationPros](https://www.aviationpros.com/aircraft-maintenance-technology/aircraft-technology/press-release/55413479/boeing-announces-new-ai-powered-platform-for-aircraft-data-management-simplifying-maintenance)). The largest engine-specific platform deal is FTAI Aviation, a CFM56 and V2500 engine MRO and exchange business. In November 2025 FTAI put its global maintenance footprint on Palantir AIP for scheduling, inventory and parts procurement ([Business Wire](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20251117324507/en/Palantir-and-FTAI-Aviation-Enter-Strategic-Partnership-to-Accelerate-the-Transformation-of-Aircraft-Engine-Maintenance-Repair-and-Exchange)).

**M&E and MRO software vendors** build AI into their own suites, and the features cluster on documents, defects and procurement:

- IFS describes an agent that turns ADs and service bulletins into compliance job cards and claims processing is more than 70% faster. This comes from a single secondary source ([ePlane AI news](https://www.eplaneai.com/news/ifs-introduces-industrial-ai-for-aviation-technicians)).
- Ramco's August 2026 deal with the engine shop Pem-Air includes a Service Bulletin Agent, GenAI assistants and corrective-action suggestions based on prior resolutions ([Asian Aviation](https://asianaviation.com/posts/pem-air-taps-ramco-systems-for-engine-mro-software)).
- Veryon AIRE recommends fixes drawn from more than 80 million pooled maintenance events ([Veryon](https://veryon.com/press-media/veryon-aire-redefines-aviation-maintenance-with-ai-powered-intelligence-across-the-entire-fleet)).
- Lufthansa Technik's AVIATAR TRE groups repeat logbook defects at more than 20 airlines but leaves the recommendation to the engineer ([DOM](https://dommagazine.com/node/304194)).
- AMOS, the most common M&E system at large airlines, announced no AI feature in its May to October 2026 news ([Swiss-AS](https://www.swiss-as.com/news-events/news)).

The strongest reported engine result is Delta TechOps' APEX, which raised material-demand forecast accuracy from 60% to over 90%. APEX serves shop-visit material planning, not the on-wing decision ([Delta TechOps](https://deltatechops.com/awn-recognizes-innovative-transformative-engine-maintenance-operation-at-delta-techops/)).

**The three products in the team's competitor line come from outside aviation.** IBM's Maximo Assistant became generally available with MAS 9.1 on 25 June 2025. It is a paid add-on that answers questions "without even leaving Maximo" ([IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/blog-enhanced-maximo-streamlines-workforce-efficiency,-investment-planning,-and-facilities-management-introduces-gen-ai-assistant)). Siemens sells a GenAI Maintenance Copilot on top of Senseye, its Azure-hosted predictive-maintenance service, and claims pilots cut reactive maintenance time by 25% ([ETMM](https://www.etmm-online.com/siemens-industrial-copilot-ai-powered-maintenance-solution-a-76790d2aebb90810d857abf89c94c051/)). Cognite Atlas AI builds agents on top of the Cognite Data Fusion knowledge graph ([Cognite](https://www.cognite.com/en/resources/blog/cognite-atlas-ai-september-product-release-accelerating-agentic-ai-at-scale)). This research found no named airline customer for any of their AI features. IBM's visible aviation references are airports ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/case-studies/amsterdam-airport-schiphol)). The funded aviation startups, Fleetcraft ($3.2M seed, January 2026) and Zymbly (Y Combinator W26), serve technicians with voice-to-record capture and manual lookup ([Fleetcraft](https://fleetcraft.com/fleetcraft-raises-3-2m-to-boost-aircraft-maintenance-efficiency-with-ai/); [YC](https://ycombinator.com/companies/zymbly)). Aerogility, the established tool for forecasting engine shop visits, uses multi-agent simulation and has done engine forecasting for easyJet since 2018 ([HMG Aerospace](https://www.hmgaerospace.com/news/lara/easyjet-upgrades-engine-forecasting-capabilities-with-aerogility/)).

Taken together, these groups leave one decision without a dedicated tool: the call on a single engine to keep it, inspect it or remove it. That call has to combine health data, records, spares, hangar slots and mandatory documents. The gap is real in what vendors publish, but it is only a gap in published material. OEMs may hold internal capabilities they do not describe, and Boeing's undisclosed TechOps AI Agent could land squarely on it.

## Rolls-Royce Optimiser and ePlane AeroGenie come closest, from opposite ends

**Rolls-Royce's Optimiser is the system in the market most like Cleris.** Microsoft described it on 21 Oct 2026. It splits shop-visit planning across close to 1,500 shop visits a year between an Engine Agent, a Workscope Agent, Facility Agents and a Supplier Agent, coordinated by an orchestrating Optimiser ([Microsoft UK Stories](https://ukstories.microsoft.com/features/rolls-royce-using-ai-agents-to-help-keep-more-aircraft-flying-for-longer/)). That is the same breakdown as Cleris's fault mode and task, parts and hangar slot. It confirms the problem framing, and it rules out any claim that multi-agent engine planning is new. The differences are just as clear:

- Optimiser serves Rolls-Royce's own planners and shop network.
- Rolls-Royce moved more than 40 business systems into Azure as a central store to build it.
- It is still in development.
- Its benefit is described only as "materially reduced" effort, with no number.
- Nothing published describes evidence IDs, rule gates or remaining-life ranges.

**ePlane AI's AeroGenie overlaps on architecture.** It turns natural-language questions into SQL over AMOS, TRAX, IFS, Ramco, SAP S/4HANA and Snowflake, shows the SQL and lets users edit it. It also states that it "does not access or interact with your actual data" ([ePlane AI](https://www.eplaneai.com/blog/aerogenie-ask-your-aviation-data-anything)). That is close enough to Cleris's read-only federated query that "we sit on your existing systems" cannot be the differentiator. AeroGenie answers analytics questions. It claims 98.7% accuracy on 73,000 validation questions with no methodology, operates a waitlist and names no customers. It does not produce a decision, apply rules, or check numbers against evidence.

Three more products cover parts of Cleris's assurance story. Palantir AIP routes model actions through ontology objects that can be "staged for human approval", and FTAI uses it in engine MRO. The data must first be integrated into Foundry ([FDE Academy](https://fde.academy/blog/palantir-aip-explained-what-it-is-and-how-fdes-use-it); [Palantir](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/platform-overview/overview/index.html)). C3 AI uses the strongest traceability language in the market: answers that "trace back to source documents and data for every insight", an "I don't know" threshold, and "hallucination-free" output. No independent audit of these claims was found ([C3 AI](https://c3.ai/why-c3-generative-ai/)). GE's ABM.ai gives the clearest incumbent statement that a human holds authority over removal timing ([GE deck](https://www.geaerospace.com/sites/default/files/2025-ge-aerospace-product-and-technology-update.pdf)). None of these features is new on its own. What is new in Cleris is **a decision brief where code makes the pass/fail call, and where the catch rate of that code has been measured.**

## Code-verified claims are the white space no vendor documents

This research reviewed more than 30 products. None of them publicly documents any of the following:

- calibrated remaining-life ranges,
- two independent remaining-life estimates,
- an evidence ID on every claim,
- deterministic gates that decide whether AI output passes.

IBM's AI Service documentation does not mention citations, guardrails or approval, even though the 9.2 assistant can "complete tasks on their behalf" ([IBM Docs](https://www.ibm.com/docs/SSRHPA_cd/appsuite/admin/c_ctr_ai_integrattion_manage.html)). Siemens's launch material does not address explainability ([ETMM](https://www.etmm-online.com/siemens-industrial-copilot-ai-powered-maintenance-solution-a-76790d2aebb90810d857abf89c94c051/)). Cognite's guardrails are permissions plus a confirmation step before a tool runs. That controls actions; it does not check whether answers are correct ([Cognite](https://www.cognite.com/en/resources/blog/cognite-atlas-ai-september-product-release-accelerating-agentic-ai-at-scale)). GE's monitoring page says nothing about how its analytics reach conclusions or how uncertain they are ([GE Aerospace](https://www.geaerospace.com/collaborative-insight)).

Cleris's results here are measured, not asserted. Across 200 active engines:

- **2,907 of 2,907 claims cite evidence IDs that resolve.**
- All 1,897 claims that state a quantity cite model, query, rule or case-memory evidence computed in code.
- No clean claim fails the number check ([Cleris evaluation report](../evaluation/report.md)).
- The deterministic gates caught **311 of 311 seeded faulty briefs across 16 fault types**, with zero false alarms on clean briefs.
- The input checks graded **116 of 116 corrupted-input runs** at the expected severity, and raised no data-quality flag on any of the 200 clean runs ([Cleris evaluation report](../evaluation/report.md)).

The most persuasive result is a negative one. With the gates bypassed, the Nemotron 3 Ultra critic confirmed only **3 of 41** seeded faults through a challenge that code could check, and code refuted **147 of its 150** challenges ([LLM critic report](../evaluation/llm_critic_report.md)). That result shows directly why the model only proposes and the code decides.

**On remaining life, Cleris treats disagreement between its two methods as a warning sign.** Method A (LightGBM) has an RMSE of 12.13 cycles. Method B (health-index extrapolation) is much worse overall at 22.31, but accurate near failure. An absolute disagreement rule of 20 cycles flags 27% of engines. On those engines, method A's error averages 11.61 cycles, against 7.48 on the rest ([evaluation report](../evaluation/report.md); [RUL report](../models/artifacts/turbofan_cmapss/rul_report.json)). Conformalised quantile regression raises the coverage of method A's 10th–90th percentile range on the active fleet from 73% to 79.5%, against an 80% target ([RUL report](../models/artifacts/turbofan_cmapss/rul_report.json)). This follows current research. A 2026 IJPHM paper applies asymmetric conformalised quantile regression to C-MAPSS, reaches 88–92% coverage at a 90% target, and argues that "even precise point predictions have limited value without dependable uncertainty estimates" ([Robinson, IJPHM 2026](https://papers.phmsociety.org/index.php/ijphm/article/view/4724)). The evaluation protocols differ, so Cleris's RMSE should not be ranked against published figures.

The decisions err on the side of safety by design. All **45 engines that fail within 15 days were sent for removal, swap or repair**, none was told to keep flying, and the planning bound sat at or below true remaining life for 92% of engines. The cost is conservatism: of the 102 engines with more than 40 days left, 41 were sent for inspection and one was pulled early ([evaluation report](../evaluation/report.md)).

These design choices match the direction of European regulation. EASA's June 2026 Proposed Issue 03 concept paper does four relevant things ([EASA Concept Paper Proposed Issue 03](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/143701/en)):

- It names "the maintenance engineer" as an audience owed operational explainability.
- It lists "sources used by the AI-based system for decision-making" as part of an explanation.
- It expects a confidence indication based on uncertainty quantification.
- It allows large off-the-shelf models such as LLMs only at the lowest assurance level.

Its maintenance example is a CAMO corrosion tool that outputs a predicted state plus a time to inspect. That sits at Level 1, where "decisions are taken by the end user" and the existing responsibility scheme is unaffected. A 2026 study of aero-engine diagnosis found that LLM-written reports hallucinated mainly by citing sensor channels that were absent from the evidence ([arXiv 2604.22777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22777)). That is the failure Cleris's number and driver checks target. This finding comes from a search excerpt, not a full reading of the paper.

Three limits keep these results honest:

- **The 311/311 result is a self-test.** The same team wrote the faults and the gates, three fault types have six or fewer samples, and the first version of the gates missed three fault types until the evaluation exposed them ([README](../README.md)).
- **The number check is permissive by design.** It allows rounding tolerances and sums or differences of headline values, so it stops numbers with no source; it does not prove each number is semantically correct ([challenges.py](../assurance/challenges.py)).
- **The 97% fault-mode accuracy is scored against a proxy label** derived from the direction of one sensor ([fault-mode report](../models/artifacts/turbofan_cmapss/fault_mode_report.json)).

## Prototype status, regulation and contracts bound the claim

Cleris is a hackathon prototype built between 22 and 27 Oct 2026. It runs on NASA's simulated C-MAPSS telemetry (two fleets, two fault modes) with synthetic maintenance, spares, slot and document data. The README says this plainly, but neither the repo nor the pitch carries a certification statement or a "not for operational use" disclaimer ([README](../README.md)). The product line "MRO clearance assistant" borrows regulated vocabulary, as do the phrases "Cleris writes that clearance" and "the release the engineer signs" ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)).

Under US rules, the approving person's signature, certificate number and certificate type constitute the return to service ([14 CFR 43.9](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/43.9)). The air carrier stays "primarily responsible" for engine airworthiness even when it contracts maintenance out ([14 CFR 121.363](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/121.363)). In Europe, authorised certifying staff issue the certificate of release to service ([EASA](https://www.easa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/dfu/CRS%20policy%20171215.pdf)). Cleris's Approve button drafts a work order and records no signer identity ([writeback.py](../tools/writeback.py)). It prepares a release decision; it does not issue one.

Binding AI rules are not yet in force. NPA 2025-07 covers data-driven machine learning at Levels 1 and 2. The follow-on NPA that would bring the framework into maintenance rules was planned for 2026 but had not appeared by October 2026 ([EASA NPA](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/newsroom-and-events/news/easas-first-regulatory-proposal-artificial-intelligence-aviation-now-open); [EASA AI publications](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/publication-types/artificial-intelligence)).

The business case is real at industry level but unmeasured for Cleris. IATA and Oliver Wyman put excess engine-leasing costs at **$2.6bn in 2025**. Shop visits have stretched from the normal 30–60 days to more than 300 days in some cases, and each extra 10 days of turnaround adds roughly $0.8bn in industry leasing cost ([IATA/Oliver Wyman](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/85b59d951fc04c1c83fa2aab47824300/reviving-the-commercial-aircraft-supply-chain.pdf)). Short-term LEAP and GTF leases passed **$6,500 a day** ([Airways](https://www.airwaysmag.com/new-post/airline-engine-bills-rise-groundings-ease)). A better-timed removal is worth six figures or more. Cleris, however, has no figure for time saved, removals avoided or cost, and the hackathon judges explicitly discount "unsupported claims of value" (Overview (hackathon Overview)).

Adoption barriers point the same way. In McKinsey's 2023 MRO survey, 84% of respondents named data accessibility and quality as the top barrier to digital deployment ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/charts/ai-wings-for-fleet-fixes)). In Oliver Wyman's 2026 survey, 58% still describe their AI work as experimental ([Oliver Wyman](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/apr/aviation-mro-labor-and-material-supply-chain-paradigm.html)). Cleris's input-plausibility gate catches bad data at the point of use, but reading data in place does not clean it.

Contracts also shape who the buyer is. Rolls-Royce TotalCare and P&W EngineWise Comprehensive come bundled with engine orders, such as Wizz Air's and Philippine Airlines' ([Aviation Business News](https://www.aviationbusinessnews.com/mro/latest-news-mro/paris-air-show-2025-wizz-air-and-pratt-whitney-agree-gtf-engines-deal/); [Aviation Business News](https://www.aviationbusinessnews.com/farnborough-international-airshow-2026/fia-2026-rolls-royce-lands-pal-a350-1000-trent-xwb-97-engine-order-and-care-deal)). Under those contracts the OEM carries the engine risk and often controls removal timing, which shrinks the airline engineer's room to decide. The engineer with the most independent stay-or-remove authority works on engines outside OEM care: mature CFM56 and V2500 fleets, independent MROs and lessors. That is exactly where FTAI chose Palantir ([Business Wire](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20251117324507/en/Palantir-and-FTAI-Aviation-Enter-Strategic-Partnership-to-Accelerate-the-Transformation-of-Aircraft-Engine-Maintenance-Repair-and-Exchange)). This is an inference, but it shows the realistic heavyweight alternative: a multi-year platform programme, not a copilot add-on.

## Conclusion

AI that gives maintenance advice is now a commodity. Every OEM, M&E vendor and industrial platform offers some form of it, and Rolls-Royce has even built the multi-agent engine planner. What remains scarce is advice that is checked automatically, with the checking measured. Incumbents explain their advice through people: OEM diagnostic engineers and analysts behind 24/7 desks. Cleris's real proposition is to make the cross-check that today depends on those people repeatable, auditable and counted. That moves the pitch from "a smarter answer" to "an answer you can audit", which is also the direction EASA's draft guidance is taking. The niche where this matters most, engines outside OEM care contracts, is the same niche Palantir entered through FTAI. The relevant comparison is therefore the cost and time of onboarding to a platform, not copilot features.

The most counterintuitive asset in the repo is a failure: the LLM critic on its own caught 3 of 41 seeded faults. That result turns "the model proposes, code decides" from a slogan into a measured design choice, and it answers in advance the hardest question judges will ask. The team's risk runs the other way: letting regulated words ("clearance", "release"), design intent ("sits on the systems the shop already has") and accuracy on simulated data stand in for proof from the field. If the team keeps to what it has measured, Cleris has a claim that is narrow, defensible and uncommon.
